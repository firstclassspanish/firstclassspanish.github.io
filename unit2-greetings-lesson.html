UNIT 2 · GREETINGS & INTRODUCTIONS

Every conversation starts the same way.

A handful of simple, reusable phrases cover almost every greeting, goodbye, and introduction you'll need. Let's learn the pattern.

Key question

What do you actually say the moment you meet someone — and the moment you leave?

🎯 What you'll learn
  • Greetings that match the time of day (saludos)
  • Simple, everyday ways to say goodbye (despedidas)
  • How to introduce yourself and ask someone's name (presentaciones)
  • When to use informal vs. formal phrasing
💡 Why it matters

These are the very first words you'll actually say in Spanish, out loud, to a real person. Getting comfortable here builds confidence fast.

Saludos (Greetings) ☀️

Phrases that follow the clock.

Hola works any time of day — when in doubt, that's always a safe choice. But Spanish speakers often greet each other based on the time of day instead, the same way English speakers sometimes say "good morning" instead of just "hi."

Hola
Hi / Hello
any time of day
Buenos días
Good morning
until around midday
Buenas tardes
Good afternoon
midday until evening
Buenas noches
Good evening / night
evening onward
Notice buenas noches does double duty — it can mean "good evening" when you arrive, or "good night" when you leave. Context makes it clear which one you mean.
A natural follow-up to any greeting: ¿Qué tal? or ¿Cómo estás? — both mean roughly "how's it going?"

Presentaciones (Introductions) 🤝

Meeting someone new.

Meeting someone new follows a simple, repeatable pattern: share your name, ask theirs, and respond to the introduction.

A quick note on translation: some Spanish phrases match English word for word — others express the same idea in a completely different way. When that happens, you learn the phrase as a whole, understanding both what it literally says and what it actually means. Me llamo Ana is a good first example: it literally says "I call myself Ana," even though the natural English is "My name is Ana."
AnaMe llamo Ana. ¿Cómo te llamas?"My name is Ana. What's your name?"
LuisMe llamo Luis. Mucho gusto."My name is Luis. Nice to meet you."
AnaIgualmente."Likewise."
Literally, word for word: me llamo = "I call myself" · ¿cómo te llamas? = "how do you call yourself?" · mucho gusto = "much pleasure." Once you notice that pattern, the natural English translations ("my name is" / "what's your name?" / "nice to meet you") make a lot more sense.
Instead of igualmente, you'll also hear encantado (if you're male) or encantada (if you're female) — both mean roughly "delighted to meet you."

Informal vs. formal 🎭

Just like in English you might greet a friend differently than a teacher, Spanish shifts the phrasing slightly depending on who you're talking to.

Informal (tú)
Friends, family, classmates — people your own age or close to you.
¿Cómo estás? · ¿Cómo te llamas?
Formal (usted)
Teachers, strangers, elders — anyone you'd address respectfully.
¿Cómo está usted? · ¿Cómo se llama usted?
Don't worry about mastering this distinction yet — it comes from grammatical person, which gets its own full unit later. For now, just notice that both versions exist.
Go Further

Greetings vary by region

›

Everything below is optional depth — the phrases above will work almost anywhere in the Spanish-speaking world. But greetings are one of the places where local flavor shows up the most.

¿Qué onda? (Mexico)

A very casual "what's up?" heard constantly among friends in Mexico.

¿Qué onda, cómo estás? — "What's up, how's it going?"
Che (Argentina & Uruguay)

Not exactly a greeting, but a very common way to get someone's attention — similar to "hey" or "dude."

Che, ¿qué tal? — "Hey, what's up?"
A kiss on the cheek

In many Latin American countries, a light cheek kiss (sometimes paired with a hand on the shoulder) is a common greeting between friends — a handshake is more common in formal or first-time situations.
